What could a community receive under the model documents?

In-Kind Standard is a nonpartisan, educational project that gives communities and their local governments free model documents, plain explanations and public records about large projects, such as data centers, so that they can decide knowing the facts: what they could gain, and what it would cost them.

It is citizen-prepared and planned to become a charitable nonprofit. It is not a government body, a law firm, an advocacy group or a developer, and it enforces nothing. It does not say whether any project should be approved or built, and never urges a county to decide a particular way.

What a large project could provide, and where each item goes

Quoted from the public record: the homes, their lines, the cards and the labels. This page does not weigh the benefits against costs. What the model documents' standards cover, among them water, sound and generator testing, is on Model documents.

Standards

Every project must meet the county's standards.

County item

The county can charge an impact fee, or use a development agreement, to cover what is needed to handle the impacts its own impact study measures, such as backup power at county facilities.

County land lease

On land the county owns, the county also sets the rent: cash rent at full market value, with rent paid in kind possible on top.

Private agreement

Other benefits, such as computing time or training seats, can come only from a private agreement between the developer and a community group. The county is not a party to that agreement.

A county does not need any agreement to protect residents.

Three of the 17 items allowed on the public record

This project chose these three, all of which pass the In-Kind test, as examples, not a ranking.

Passing the test only means an item is not ruled out. It does not mean the item is lawful in a given county, wanted by whoever would receive it, agreed to by a developer, or worth a set amount. What the colored labels mean

County agreement, Module M2

Emergency power for county facilities

Fits provisionallyCounty itemBuilt or given once

Only as mitigation the county's impact study supports

Mitigation, sized by the county's impact study. Nothing is delivered from the project site.

What it would do

If the grid goes down for days, the emergency operations center and the other county emergency facilities the county's study names could keep their lights, cooling and radios running. At a shelter, the equipment is sized only for people evacuated because of an incident at the data center, not for hurricane sheltering. The equipment sits at the emergency facility, behind that facility's meter. The utility owns it, or the developer leases it to the county, or it is given to the county with money set aside to maintain it.

What it would do

If your school, college or clinic is near the route, the developer may offer it fiber strands directly, under a private agreement. County-run buildings, including county libraries, cannot receive them this way. This does not bring internet service to your home.

What this work does not yet have

  • No county has adopted the model documents, and no lawyer has reviewed them.
  • Their fees and mitigation amounts stay blank until studies for the county that uses them supply them, and none of those studies has been done.
  • The project has no organization of its own yet, and no fiscal sponsor (an established nonprofit that would oversee the work and hold its money) or board.

Who prepares this work

The work is prepared by one person: the project's founder, a private citizen. No one but the founder has paid for it: the founder pays its small costs himself, and the project has received no money or other gifts. AI tools help draft and check the work, and the founder reviews every item and decides what is published, except work the founder steps aside from because of a tie, meaning a connection that might be, or look like, a conflict of interest.
