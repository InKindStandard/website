How we work

How the work is made, and who it is for

In-Kind Standard is a nonpartisan, educational project that gives communities and their local governments free model documents, plain explanations and public records about large projects, such as data centers, so that they can decide knowing the facts: what they could gain, and what it would cost them.

It is citizen-prepared and planned to become a charitable nonprofit. It is not a government body, a law firm, an advocacy group or a developer, and it enforces nothing. It does not say whether any project should be approved or built, and never urges a county to decide a particular way. It takes no money or help from anyone with a direct financial stake in how its documents are written or applied, and nothing it does, funds or recommends may let the public be watched.

Who does the work

One person today, with AI tools

Today the project is the work of one person, the founder, who runs it unpaid and is bound by the Political Neutrality Policy's rules for leaders, which limit political activity. AI tools help draft and check the work, and the founder reviews every item and decides what is published, except work the founder steps aside from because of a tie, meaning a connection that might be, or look like, a conflict of interest. Such work waits until someone without the tie can prepare and check it.

The founder's ties, and the decisions on them, are listed in the founder's record of ties, on Who pays for the work. Work that is paid elsewhere is paid here, at a fair rate, once there is money for it; until then, the founder alone does it unpaid.

Not yet in place: an unpaid advisory committee to decide complaints about the founder and questions about the founder's own ties, and to review the founder's earlier decisions on them; a fiscal sponsor, an established nonprofit that would oversee the work and hold its money; a lawyer; and later a board, and a Standards Council to decide the content of the model documents.

How the work runs

Start to finish, in ten steps

Today the founder does steps 1 to 8, with AI tools that help draft and check the work; steps 9 and 10 are planned.

  1. Find the question communities face when a large project seeks to locate there.
  2. Read first: the law, the official records and what comparable bodies do, at first hand.
  3. Draft the model documents and their explanations.
  4. Check each item against its sources and run the six checks of the Neutrality Test before it goes out.
  5. Publish it free, with its known gaps stated.
  6. Explain it in plain guides and, when invited, in talks, without favoring any option.
  7. Record what counties and cities do, from their official records.
  8. Correct errors as prominently as the original; complaints about our own work go through the Complaints Policy.
  9. Review and report once a year: state law and the model documents, and a public report on neutrality, gifts and complaints, with an outside review once there is a board.
  10. Widen the published work to other states and kinds of projects: logistics facilities and automation, and Georgia and South Carolina, are coming soon.

Every known gap

  • No county has adopted the model documents, and no lawyer has reviewed them.
  • Their fees and mitigation amounts stay blank until studies for the county that uses them supply them, and none of those studies has been done.
  • The project has no organization of its own yet, and no fiscal sponsor (an established nonprofit that would oversee the work and hold its money) or board.
  • Today the founder decides the content and checks it, with AI tools. The Neutrality Test, the project's six checks that an item takes no side, is new and so far tried on only a few items; a pass shows that its checks were made, not that the result is fair.
  • Because the founder prepared and approved all the published work, every complaint about it waits, with no date set, for an advisory committee not yet formed; the founder is looking for its members. Reports of factual errors that show the official record or text are checked and, if wrong, corrected now.
  • The project's policies are drafts the founder has approved; no organization has adopted them, and no lawyer has reviewed them.
