**Known gaps.**

- No county has adopted the model documents, and no lawyer has reviewed them.
- Their fees and mitigation amounts stay blank until studies for the county that uses them supply them, and none of those studies has been done.
- The project has no organization of its own yet, and no fiscal sponsor (an established nonprofit that would oversee the work and hold its money) or board.
- Today the founder decides the content and checks it, with AI tools. The Neutrality Test, the project's six checks that an item takes no side, is new and so far tried on only a few items; a pass shows that its checks were made, not that the result is fair.
- Because the founder prepared and approved all the published work, every complaint about it waits, with no date set, for an advisory committee not yet formed; the founder is looking for its members. Reports of factual errors that show the official record or text are checked and, if wrong, corrected now.
- The project's policies are drafts the founder has approved; no organization has adopted them, and no lawyer has reviewed them.

## 1. What we are

In-Kind Standard is a nonpartisan, educational project that gives communities and their local governments free model documents, plain explanations and public records about large projects, such as data centers, so that they can decide knowing the facts: what they could gain, and what it would cost them.

It is citizen-prepared and planned to become a charitable nonprofit. It is not a government body, a law firm, an advocacy group or a developer, and it enforces nothing. It does not say whether any project should be approved or built, and never urges a county to decide a particular way. It takes no money or help from anyone with a direct financial stake in how its documents are written or applied, and nothing it does, funds or recommends may let the public be watched.

## 2. The roles we play

Today the project offers three things, all free:

1. **Model documents:** ordinances, agreements and forms that a Florida county can adapt when a large data center seeks to locate there, published as "Model Instruments for Data Center Development in Florida Counties" (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24021154).
2. **Public records:** the County Record, of what Florida counties and cities have done about data centers, each action checked against the official record, and the Screen, which shows what a community could receive from one and where Florida law limits what a county may ask for. Both are on the project's web page, "What Florida Counties Can Do About a Large Data Center" (https://screen.inkindstandard.org/).
3. **Plain explanations**, such as the web page's guides to the law and to scenarios for planners and councils, which set out each option without favoring one.

## 3. Who does the work

Today the project is the work of one person, the founder, who runs it unpaid and is bound by the Political Neutrality Policy's rules for leaders, which limit political activity. AI tools help draft and check the work, and the founder reviews every item and decides what is published, except work the founder steps aside from because of a tie, meaning a connection that might be, or look like, a conflict of interest. Such work waits until someone without the tie can prepare and check it (Conflict of Interest Policy, section 3.5). The founder's ties, and the decisions on them, are listed in "The Founder's Ties and Decisions" (the same policy, sections 3.4 and 9). That record is sent to anyone who asks and, once the project's main website is live, will be shown there. Work that is paid elsewhere is paid here, at a fair rate, once there is money for it; until then, the founder alone does it unpaid. Not yet in place: an unpaid advisory committee to decide complaints about the founder and questions about the founder's own ties, and to review the founder's earlier decisions on them; a fiscal sponsor, an established nonprofit that would oversee the work and hold its money; a lawyer; and later a board, and a Standards Council to decide the content of the model documents.

## 4. Who our work is for, and how we relate to each

| Who | What we offer | What we never do |
|---|---|---|
| Counties and other public bodies | The same free documents, records and explanations for every body | Give legal advice |
| Residents and the wider public | The same, with corrections when we are wrong and a way to complain about our own work | Take complaints about others; those go to the public body concerned, such as a county's code enforcement office |
| Anyone with a direct financial stake | The same documents and answers as anyone | Take their money or help |
| Funders and donors (none yet) | The same account of the work and its gaps; every organization that gives is named, and so is any individual who gives $5,000 or more in a year | Give a funder a say over the work, tie a gift to a developer, a record entry or a decision before a county, or promise what a county will do |
| People who help (none yet) | Fair pay once there is money; each discloses ties that touch the work and steps aside from it | Ask them to do, for free, work that is paid elsewhere, or pay anyone a share of money raised |
| Reviewers and advisers (none yet) | Named only with their agreement | Claim a review or endorsement unless it is confirmed in writing and the reviewer agrees to be named |

## 5. How the work runs, start to finish

Today the founder does steps 1 to 8, with AI tools that help draft and check the work (section 3); steps 9 and 10 are planned.

1. **Find the question** communities face when a large project seeks to locate there; today, large data centers in Florida.
2. **Read first:** the law, the official records and what comparable bodies do, at first hand.
3. **Draft** the model documents and their explanations.
4. **Check** each item against its sources and, as the neutrality policy now requires, run the six checks of the Neutrality Test before it goes out.
5. **Publish** it free, with its known gaps stated.
6. **Explain** it in plain guides and, when invited, in talks, without favoring any option.
7. **Record** what counties and cities do, from their official records.
8. **Correct** errors as prominently as the original; complaints about our own work go through the Complaints Policy.
9. **Review and report** once a year: state law and the model documents, and a public report on neutrality, gifts and complaints, with an outside review once there is a board.
10. **Widen** the published work to other states and kinds of projects once each one's law is mapped, the demand is shown and the people and money are in place, and the founder decides.

## 6. What comes next (decided plans, not work under way)

Beyond the model documents, the project plans programs to lower the cost of essentials such as food, housing, water and energy:

- **open designs and kits:** free, openly licensed designs, such as small homes and hurricane-rated greenhouses, and kits (step-by-step guides with templates) for starting local businesses, such as repair shops;
- **contests and paid commissions** to make them, each design reviewed independently before it enters the library and, as a rule, designed so that its core use keeps working without its automation;
- **an open design library**, decided in principle, showing each design in the exact version reviewed.

At first, each would need, among other conditions, a gift for that purpose and a fiscal sponsor that agrees to run it before it could start, and would be dropped if an independent evaluation found that it does not lower costs. No program includes any part of the model documents, and no gift for a program reaches them.

It also plans one paid course: a live online course of about a day on the model documents, at one published price, set before the first run, for county staff and officials and anyone else without a direct financial stake in how the documents are written or applied, with a free recording of each run published for everyone. Like the rest of the work, it explains every option, including adopting none of the documents, and gives no advice on any county's own application.

## 7. How it is paid for, and how to reach us

The founder pays the project's small costs, and the project has received no money or other gifts. It is not asking the public for gifts yet. Once there is a fiscal sponsor, gifts of money would go through it (later, through the project's own nonprofit), never in anyone's own name. Funders will be listed in the funding statement, "Who prepares this work, and who pays for it" (none so far), and so will the yearly total of any fees, such as for the course.

Questions, corrections and complaints about our work: contact@inkindstandard.org
